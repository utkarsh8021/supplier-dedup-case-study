Supplier de-duplication

"The system can only suggest a match. A person has to decide." The whole exploration is really about that handoff — how much to automate, and how to make the suggestion trustworthy enough to act on.

100K+supplier instances
6pain points named first
00 · Overview

The problem, and the tension underneath it

Problem: the same supplier gets entered into the system multiple times by different Supplier Managers, splitting spend data and purchase history across duplicate records.

Ask: design a way for a Buyer Admin to find, review, and resolve these duplicates at scale — 100K+ records.

Core tension: the system can only suggest a match. A person has to decide. Everything below is really about that handoff — how much to automate, and how to make the suggestion trustworthy enough to act on.

01 · Understanding the problem

What the brief is actually asking

Help someone trust an algorithm's guess enough to act on it, repeatedly, without breaking live purchase orders or contacts.

Every requirement in the brief exists to serve one moment — the point where an Admin looks at a group and decides what to do with it. Search, filters, bulk actions, notifications are all secondary to getting that moment right.

Merging is functionally irreversible in how it feels to the person doing it — live POs sit on these records. An unexplained "87% match" produces hesitation, not action. A percentage with no reasoning behind it fails the brief even if it's technically present.

02 · Exploration

Key points pulled from the PRD

Went through the brief and pulled out what actually shapes the design, grouped under the areas it covers.

Scale

100K+ supplier instances in the system. Duplicates form multiple groups, each tied to one supplier organization.

Detection

System auto-identifies likely duplicates, each needing a confidence score. Comparison covers legal name, status, head office, primary contact, contact email, DUNS number, supplier code, supplier manager.

Decision-making

Admin manually confirms which instances are duplicates, picks a lead/master record per group. Changes apply to everything marked, in one action.

Discovery

Admin needs to narrow down and find a specific supplier instance, not just browse groups blindly.

Communication

Option to notify the Supplier Manager responsible for an instance, and/or contacts at the supplier.

03 · From the Buyer Admin's side

Main pain points

Before designing anything, tried to sit in the Admin's seat and name what actually makes this task hard for them day to day. These points became the actual brief designed against — more useful than the literal requirement list, because they explain why each requirement exists.

"I don't know why the system thinks these are duplicates."

A bare percentage gives no reason to trust it, so the safe move is to do nothing — which defeats the point of automation.

"I don't know which record is safe to keep."

Several records, one has to become the source of truth going forward — getting it wrong means broken purchase orders later.

"I'm not sure if this one is a duplicate or not."

Real cases sit in a grey zone — need a way to leave something undecided instead of forcing a yes/no.

"There are thousands of these — I can't review them one by one forever."

Sheer volume means the Admin needs to triage fast, not review carefully every single time.

"If I make a mistake, can I undo it?"

Merging feels permanent and risky with no visible safety net.

"Who do I tell if the data itself is wrong?"

Sometimes the problem isn't duplication at all — a record just has outdated or incorrect information, and there's no obvious path to flag that from here.

04 · Prioritising the problem

The core problem, stated in one line

Help the Admin trust a suggested match enough to act on it — quickly, and without breaking anything downstream.

Every other requirement in the brief is only useful if this works. Search, filters, bulk actions, notifications — all of them assume the Admin already trusts the suggestion in front of them. This is also the piece with no existing pattern to copy: search bars and filter dropdowns are solved problems, but explaining an algorithm's confidence in a way that earns trust is not — so it deserved the most design time, not the least.

PriorityFeatureReasoning
1 · CoreConfidence score + explanationNothing else matters if this isn't trusted
1 · CoreLead selection + record comparisonThe actual decision being made — inseparable from trust
2 · EnablingA way to leave a record undecidedNeeded so the core decision isn't forced into a false yes/no
2 · EnablingStatus that reflects real progressTrust in the decision must extend to trust in the tracking
3 · ScaleSearch, filters, sort, virtual scrollingMakes the core flow usable at 1,200+ groups — but only once the flow itself is right
3 · ScaleBulk mergeA shortcut on top of the core decision, restricted to zero-judgment cases
4 · SupportingNotificationsCommunicates the outcome of a decision — doesn't change how it's made
4 · SupportingReversibility / undoA safety net around the core action, not the action itself

The judgment call this reflects: treated confidence + decision-making as the product, and search/filters/bulk/notifications as infrastructure around it — necessary, but secondary. The opposite of working the brief top to bottom as a checklist: spend the least time on the parts that were easiest to get right, and the most on the one requirement with no clear "correct" answer.

05 · What I need more clarity on

Open questions, and the assumptions made to move forward

Points the brief doesn't answer, plus what was assumed to keep designing instead of stalling on them.

Open questions

  • ?No logic given for how the confidence score should actually be calculated
  • ?No answer for what happens to merged records' own data — overwritten, deleted, kept?
  • ?Notify is written at group level, but phrased in the singular ("the Supplier Manager responsible for a supplier instance") — suggests per-record, not just per-group
  • ?Nothing about undo or reversibility, despite the action being effectively permanent
  • ?The brief describes why duplicates happen (multiple Supplier Managers, overlapping entry) but only asks for cleanup, never anything to prevent the next one

Assumptions made to fill the gaps

  • →A DUNS Number is generally reliable when present, since it's the strongest identifier available
  • →Roughly 2–4% of records are duplicates, since the brief gives no real figure and a much lower rate would make the scale problem trivial
  • →A supplier hierarchy already exists for linking related entities, since the brief doesn't say either way
  • →The review and grouping thresholds are reasonable starting points, flagged as things to calibrate once there's real usage data

07 · Working out the logic

How the groups, score, and decisions actually work

A. How groups form

Every pair of records compared on name, DUNS number, head office, supplier code, email domain. Similar pairs get linked; links chain — if A matches B and B matches C, all three become one group even if A and C never directly matched. Only records that clear a threshold ever form a group — most of the 100K+ never appear in the queue at all.

B. The confidence score — three passes to get right

1
Failed

Plain weighted average

Fails on an obvious real-world case: "IBM New York" and "IBM London" — identical name, different company — would score falsely high.

2
Refined

Split hard identifiers from soft attributes

Hard identifier: DUNS number, supplier code — issued externally, a match means something real. Soft attributes: name, location, email domain — circumstantial only.

3
The real gap

Found while writing the explanation

Both records have a DUNS number, and the numbers don't match. The original model gave this no penalty — just no shortcut to a high score. That's wrong: a DUNS mismatch is strong evidence of two separate companies, not neutral. Fixed by adding a cap, not a penalty multiplier — multipliers compound into numbers nobody can sanity-check.

Final simplified rule

  • Same DUNS on both95% · done
  • Legal name match50 / 40 / 25 / 10
  • Head office — same city+30
  • Head office — same country+15
  • Email domain match+20
  • Cap: DUNS present but different≤ 40
  • Cap: different country≤ 50
  • Cap: no DUNS on either side≤ 85
  • Below 30 points totalnot grouped

C. Explaining the score — a decision to simplify twice

First version showed the full math breakdown — points, weights, caps. Cut it: nobody reviewing 1,200 groups wants to check arithmetic, they need to believe the number, not audit it. Replaced with plain findings and one sentence.

Why 40% — probably not the same company

✓ / ✗ / – mark agrees / disagrees / no evidence either way. A heading translates the number, and a closing line points to an action.

D. Choosing the lead record

System pre-selects, Admin can override. Order: Active status → most complete record → most open POs → most recently updated → oldest. Most open POs matters most in practice — fewer purchase orders need re-pointing after merge.

E. What merging actually changes

Merged records keep their own data — never overwritten by the lead's values. Overwriting would hide the evidence in the comparison table, misrepresent what actually happens to the data, and produce several identical records instead of one clear one. The lead absorbs values only for fields it was blank on — never overwrites a value that's already there. Old codes/names still resolve to the lead on search, so historical POs don't break.

F. Marking duplicates — kept it to a single checkbox

Each row has one Duplicate checkbox, ticked by default for every non-lead record. Untick it to leave that record out of the merge — no separate flow, no reason required.

This replaced an earlier version that asked for a reason every time a record was excluded. It solved a real problem in theory but added a confirmation step to something that's really just a selection, not a commitment. The commitment happens at Merge, not before it.

G. Status — worked out from what's actually been done, not clicked by hand

Status reflects the real state of the group, not a manual flag. The point of keeping Reviewed and Merged separate: reviewing is a judgment call, merging is an irreversible action. Collapsing them into one status would hide whether anything had actually been committed yet.

Pending nothing done yet
Reviewed unchecked anything that isn't a duplicate, but hasn't hit Merge yet
Merged the merge action has been run for this group
Review skipped deliberately parked for later
08 · Designing for scale

Built and tested at 1,240 groups

Not 6 — so scale problems were real rather than theoretical.

Virtual scrolling

Only visible rows rendered.

Full-width filter bar

Freeing the side rail to be a pure list.

Sort by open PO exposure

Default — the only ordering with a real business case: duplicate groups sitting on live POs are actively distorting spend reporting right now.

Match quality chips

Clear match, Pending review, In progress, Merged — so the shape of the backlog is visible at a glance, not just a total count.

Keyboard navigation (J/K)

For clearing a long backlog without constant mouse trips.

Bulk merge, gated

Strictly to DUNS-matched groups only — the one case with zero judgment required.

09 · Deciding what needs confirmation

Friction scales with how easy a mistake is to notice

A bad merge is loud — wrong PO, angry manager, numbers look off. Self-correcting. Unchecking a duplicate is quiet and reversible — nothing commits until Merge is actually clicked, so it doesn't need its own confirmation.

ActionConfirms?Asks
Merge selectedYesWhich record survives, what folds in, what moves, who's notified
Notify supplier managerYesConfirms nothing else in the group changes
Skip groupNoNothing lost — reversible from the queue
Tick/untick Duplicate, change the leadNoJust a selection — nothing commits until Merge
10 · Ideas explored and dropped

What didn't survive scrutiny

CUT

A reason-picker for excluding a record from a merge

Built this first — untick a record, and it asked why (different entity, subsidiary, wrong data, etc). Dropped it because it treated a selection like a commitment.

A single checkbox does the job; the reasoning only matters once you actually merge.
CUT

A manual "mark reviewed" button

No honest use case survived scrutiny. If status is derived from what's actually been done, a manual override just lets someone lie about progress.

CUT

Letting the Admin pick a field value from any record to fix the lead

Solved a real problem — what if the surviving record has wrong data? — but meant editing supplier data from a de-dup screen.

A job that belongs with the Supplier Manager, not here.
CUT

Letting the Admin create a brand-new lead record

Rejected outright. This is the exact behaviour that causes duplicates in the first place, and a new record has no PO history.

11 · Along the way

Initial design explorations and confusions

Some decisions weren't clean from the start — small, specific UX questions that needed working through before the final screens held together.

How much reasoning to show for the score

Weighed two options: collapse the reasoning into a short one-line tag next to the score (like "DUNS mismatch +3"), or lay out the full reasoning inside the table cell itself. Landed on both — a compact tag in the cell for scanning, with the full plain-language breakdown available on demand.

Filters as pills, not a dropdown

Considered tucking review status into a dropdown to save space. Decided on pills instead — it's important enough, and used often enough by Admins, that it deserves to stay visible and one click away, not hidden behind a menu.

Ticking a duplicate shouldn't ask for anything

The open question: should every tick of the Duplicate checkbox fire a confirmation, or a notification? Decided against it — the tick is reversible, so it doesn't need to interrupt. Once every non-lead record is ticked, the Admin can go straight to "Notify duplicates" in one action.

What search actually searches

The open question: does search operate on groups, or on the instances inside them? Concluded it needs to reach into the instances within every group — legal name, DUNS, supplier code, contact — so an Admin can jump straight to the one record they're after instead of hunting through groups first.

Marking what's different from the lead

Needed a way to show, at a glance, which fields on a duplicate diverge from the lead. Settled on a soft yellow background on any cell that differs, paired with a blue background on the lead row itself — so both "the record being kept" and "what doesn't match it" read instantly.

12 · Final design — summary

Where it landed

  • 1Two-panel layout — a group queue on the left, a comparison view on the right.
  • 2Comparison table shows all data points side by side, with a single Duplicate checkbox per row.
  • 3Confidence explained in plain language, not shown as math.
  • 4Every non-lead record ends up in one of three places: merged into the lead, left unticked for a later look, or ticked and waiting to be merged.
  • 5Status derived automatically — Pending, Reviewed, Merged, or Review skipped — never set by hand.
  • 6Notifications available per record and per group, matching the brief's actual wording.
  • 7Merging and notifying confirm; selecting and skipping don't — friction is intentional, not uniform.

13 · Insights and reflection

What this process actually taught

01

The hardest problems weren't on the requirements list — the scoring logic and the status model both needed real design thinking the brief never asked for directly.

02

Naming the pain points before designing anything changed the priority order entirely. The literal requirement list treats every bullet as equally important; the pain points made it obvious that trust in the confidence score was the one thing everything else depended on.

03

The best bug was found by trying to explain the system, not by testing it — the DUNS-mismatch scoring gap only became visible when forced to write a plain-language reason for a score.

04

Removing features mattered more than adding them. The reason-picker for exclusions is the clearest example — built, used, then cut because it treated a simple selection like a serious decision. That kind of self-correction says more than a longer feature list would.

05

The underlying idea holding the whole design together: the system proposes, the person decides — every suggestion carries a reason, and nothing commits until the Admin actually merges. That's what makes it possible to trust automation enough to act on it at volume.
